### 📰 Issue 1: July 2026
#### **The Piece about Peace: Reimagining the Security Paradigm: From Guns to Governance**

Amina starts her day in the quiet dark before dawn, walking the rows of her farm in Zangon Kataf, Southern Kaduna. She looks closely at her maize crop, checking for damage. Two years ago, this daily routine felt heavy with worry. Back then, a single incident of stray cattle ruining a neighbor's field sparked a chain reaction of anger, rumors, and violence. By the time the military arrived in heavy patrol vehicles, the barns were already ashes. Amina and her children spent the next month sleeping on a cold school floor, waiting for food handouts.

When conflict flares up in Nigeria or anywhere else on the African continent, governments respond in a very predictable way. They buy newer weapons, declare curfews, and send in troops. Massive amounts of money are diverted from public funds to buy combat gear and pay for troop movements.

But the numbers from the Beyond# Human Security Observatory tell a different story. This militarized strategy is not working. High-pressure military checkpoints do not stop kidnappers from moving through the bush, and fighter jets cannot settle disputes over farmland. When we rely solely on soldiers, we are trying to fix deep developmental issues with tactical force. True safety does not come from a state's arsenal. It comes from the stability of the people living in these communities.

We need to talk about what security actually means. The standard focus is almost always on protecting borders and governments. But real safety has several different layers that affect daily life. People need economic security, meaning a way to earn a living. They need food security to get through the lean season, health security to access basic clinics, and environmental security to handle unpredictable weather. They also need personal safety from violence, community security to live peacefully alongside their neighbors, and political security to know their basic rights are respected.

When you look at conflict this way, the divide between security and development goes away. If a young person cannot find a job and struggles to eat, joining a local gang or an armed group starts to look like a way to survive. If a pastoralist family loses their grazing land to dry weather, they have to move south, which leads to disputes with local farmers. These crises start with developmental problems, yet the government treats them as military issues. By the time the troop carriers arrive, the damage is already done.

This militarized mindset shows up clearly in our budgets. Across West Africa, spending on defense routinely swallows up funds that should go to schools, clinics, and farming support. This fiscal choice creates a problematic cycle. The government ignores rural areas, so the local economy collapses and people lose hope. Some turn to illegal economies or armed groups for income. In response, the state spends even more on weapons, further cutting the funds available for development.

We cannot buy our way out of this with weapons. For instance, the high rate of out-of-school children in Northern Nigeria is a major security risk. A child who never goes to school is largely cut off from the modern economy, making them easy targets for recruitment later in life. Guns do not teach children how to read, and military vehicles cannot bring water to dry fields.

In Amina's community, we tried a different approach. We helped set up the Conflict Early Warning and Response System, or CEWRS. Instead of waiting for a clash to happen and calling the army, the community built its own warning network using local monitors.

These monitors come from all walks of life: farmers, pastoralists, women, and youth groups. They keep an eye out for early signs of tension, like stolen cattle, crop damage, or toxic rumors. If they see something, they send an encrypted text message to a local peace committee. The committee verifies the alert and steps in to resolve the issue immediately. If cows eat a farmer's maize, the committee helps negotiate fair compensation before tempers flare.

Over twelve months, this community system handled 143 alerts, responding in under twenty minutes on average. By dealing with problems early, they reduced communal violence casualties by 42% in that area. They did this without weapons, relying instead on communication and community trust.

If we want lasting peace, we have to change where we put our resources. We need to invest in farming, rural schools, and basic healthcare instead of importing expensive weapons. Peace is not something you enforce with a troop deployment: it is what happens when a society protects its people from hunger, sickness, and exclusion. This monthly column, "The Piece about Peace", is about those human stories. The path to safety is built on good governance, not guns.

### 📰 Issue 2: August 2026
#### **The Piece about Peace: The Anatomy of Elusive Peace: Social Inequality, Elite Capture, and Political Exclusion**

Musa is 23 years old, has a computer science diploma, and spends his days sitting under a neem tree in Jigawa State. He spends hours scrolling through his phone, talking with friends, and waiting. Nearby, massive political billboards show wealthy candidates promising a bright future. Musa looks at the posters, then at the dirt road in front of him. That road leads to a clinic with no medicine and a school where children sit on the floor. He wonders why the country's wealth never seems to travel down his street.

When people discuss why peace is so hard to find in Nigeria and across Africa, they often talk about religion or ethnicity. While these identities are sometimes used to stir up trouble, they are not the real cause. The true drivers of conflict are social inequality, political exclusion, and elite capture.

In many places, the state behaves like an extraction tool for the wealthy rather than a service provider for everyone. A small elite controls the national resources, whether it is oil, minerals, or agricultural exports, while the rest of the population is left behind. This inequality is about more than just income. It is about a lack of access to clean water, decent schools, and fair justice. When people see the state protecting the powerful while ignoring the poor, they lose faith in the system. Under these conditions, peace cannot take root.

The data from the Beyond# Multidimensional Poverty Index shows how regional inequality feeds this instability. The divide is very clear. In southern states like Anambra and Ekiti, multidimensional poverty rates are under 25%. In northern states like Jigawa, Bauchi, and Borno, those rates climb above 75%.

This gap is the result of decades of centralized spending and the neglect of rural economies. The impact of this neglect is clear on our security dashboards. In the Northwest, high youth unemployment and failing schools have left a generation of young people with very few options, and many have turned to organized banditry. In the Northeast, decades of economic isolation created the exact conditions that allowed insurgent groups to recruit young people.

You cannot secure a region by force when the people living there are struggling just to survive. The statistics on our dashboard show that ignoring regional inequality makes instability inevitable.

This problem is kept alive by elite capture. The political system often works on patronage, where office is used for personal gain. Elections become intense, high-stakes contests because losing means losing access to state funds. To keep their grip on power, elites often weaken the public institutions that are supposed to hold them accountable. This decay leaves ordinary citizens with no way to seek justice.

At the same time, youth and women are systematically shut out of the political process. With no peaceful way to influence decisions, marginalized groups are driven toward civil unrest, protests, or armed resistance.

We are seeing the results of this exclusion across West Africa. The recent military coups in Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger were fueled by deep public anger against political elites who failed to protect citizens or provide basic services. While military rule is not the answer, the public support for these coups shows how desperate people are for a system that actually serves them.

Africa has the youngest population in the world, with more than 60% of its people under the age of 25. This could be a massive engine for growth. Instead, because of a lack of reform, it has become a major source of tension.

In Nigeria and across West Africa, millions of young people finish school every year with almost no job opportunities. They are governed by political leaders who are often generations older, creating a massive disconnect. Young people feel shut out of both the economy and the political system. They see a world where who you know matters more than what you can do, and where peaceful protests are sometimes met with state force. 

When youth are denied both economic opportunities and a political voice, alternative power structures, like criminal networks or migration, start to look like their only options. Peace will remain out of reach as long as we treat young people as a threat to be managed rather than a resource to be empowered.

To change this, we have to rebuild the connection between the state and its citizens. We need to decentralize resource spending, moving public investments toward rural farming, clean water, and primary schools. We must lower the barriers for women and youth to participate in politics, and we need to strengthen the institutions that hold leaders accountable.

Peace is not just the absence of violence. It is a social agreement that exists when citizens feel the system is fair and their future is secure. Until we address the inequality that shapes daily life for Musa and millions like him, the search for stability will remain unfinished.

### 📰 Issue 3: September 2026
#### **The Piece about Peace: Borderlands, Ecologies, and Arms: The West African Fragility Equation**

Halima used to walk four kilometers every afternoon under the hot Niger State sun to get water from a muddy stream. The walk was exhausting, and the water made her children sick. At the water source, arguments were common as women struggled to fill their containers first. In this dry landscape, water was not just hard to find: it was a constant source of tension.

Halima's daily struggle is connected to a larger network of instability across West Africa. The challenges facing Nigeria and its neighbors are linked by climate change, porous borders, and the flow of small arms.

Conflict in this region does not stay within national borders. Insurgencies in northeastern Nigeria quickly spread into Cameroon, Chad, and Niger. Banditry in the northwest is fueled by weapons smuggled across the desert, and dry weather pushes pastoralist communities across borders, leading to disputes with farmers far from home. To build peace, we have to look at the regional factors that link the sub-continent together.

Ecological degradation is one of the most powerful conflict drivers in the region. Climate change is already shifting livelihoods and security. The shrinking of Lake Chad is a clear example.

Over the last sixty years, Lake Chad has lost nearly 90% of its water. This ecological collapse has disrupted the lives of millions of fishermen, farmers, and pastoralists. The loss of jobs created an economic void, which extremist groups exploited by offering money and support to young people.

Further north, desertification is forcing pastoralists to migrate south in search of water and pasture. The resulting friction between pastoralists and farmers is often reported as a cultural or religious conflict, but it is fundamentally a dispute over resources driven by climate change. Sending in soldiers might stop the violence temporarily, but it does not solve the problem of resource scarcity.

This vulnerability is made worse by porous borders. The borders of West Africa are largely artificial, drawn during the colonial era without regard for local communities or geography. Today, these borders are highly porous, making it difficult for under-resourced border agencies to monitor them.

Since the collapse of the Libyan state in 2011, millions of weapons have entered the regional black market, flowing south into the Sahel and West Africa. This access to small arms has made local disputes much more lethal. A disagreement over land that might once have been resolved through local mediation can now escalate quickly into armed violence.

Additionally, porous borders have allowed illicit economies, such as smuggling and illegal mining, to grow. These networks generate revenue that is used to buy weapons and challenge local authorities. Borderland communities, often isolated from their national capitals, are left to navigate these security challenges on their own, sometimes forming alliances with armed actors for protection.

To address these challenges, Beyond# has focused on localized, community-managed resource projects.

In Halima's village in Niger State, Beyond# helped establish a solar-powered water purification pilot managed by a local, women-led committee. The community pays a small fee for water to fund maintenance and security. By giving local women control over the resource, water-borne illnesses decreased by 85%, and local water disputes dropped by 85%. The project showed that community governance of resources can prevent conflict.

In Kano State, where high youth unemployment presents a security risk, Beyond# established solar-powered greenhouse hubs. Thirty young people were trained in hydroponic farming and supply-chain tracking. By reducing water usage by 60% and increasing youth incomes by an average of 140%, the project provided stable, climate-resilient livelihoods, reducing the vulnerability of youth to recruitment by illicit networks.

Solving the West African fragility equation requires regional cooperation that goes beyond military alliances. We need a coordinated strategy.

First, we must support transboundary resource management, coordinating climate adaptation and water conservation across the Sahel. Second, we need to invest in borderland communities, providing schools, clinics, and infrastructure to integrate them into the regional economy. Third, we must coordinate arms control, implementing regional programs to track and reduce the flow of small arms. Fourth, we need to scale up community-managed resource projects to protect local livelihoods from climate shocks.

Enduring stability in West Africa will not be achieved by military force alone. It will be built through regional partnerships that address ecological changes, secure borderland livelihoods, and replace the flow of weapons with the flow of opportunity.
